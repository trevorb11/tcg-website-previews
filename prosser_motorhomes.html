Motorhome Classes

Know What You're Getting Into

The biggest mistake I see first-time buyers make is picking a class based on looks. Let me give you the real rundown — what each class is actually like to live with.

Class A

Class A Motorhomes

26 - 45 ft | Full-Size Coach

This is the big dog. A Class A gives you a real living room, a real bedroom, a full kitchen — the works. If you're doing extended trips or spending summers up in the Northwoods, this is how you do it right. Just know what you're signing up for: these things are wide, and parallel parking in downtown Green Bay isn't happening.

Best For

  • Extended trips or full-timing
  • Couples who want real space
  • Campgrounds with full hookups
  • Retiring and hitting the road hard
$100K - $350K+ | New MSRP Range
Class B

Class B Motorhomes

17 - 23 ft | Camper Van

Class B vans are great for couples — genuinely. You can park them anywhere, drive them like a normal vehicle, and slip into spots at Devil's Lake or Peninsula State Park that a Class A could never touch. The Coachmen Beyond has a Murphy bed that actually works, which matters because most don't. But if you've got kids, honestly, look at a Class C instead. You'll thank me.

Best For

  • Couples and solo travelers
  • Boondocking and tight campgrounds
  • Weekend trips to Door County or Devil's Lake
  • People who want to drive it every day
$90K - $160K+ | New MSRP Range
Class C

Class C Motorhomes

20 - 33 ft | Mid-Size

Honestly, most of the families I work with end up here, and they're never mad about it. The over-cab bunk is perfect for kids, the driving profile is manageable for first-timers, and you get real living space without the sticker shock of a Class A. This is the rig I'd hand to my brother-in-law if he asked what to buy for summer trips to Lake Geneva with the family.

Best For

  • Families with kids
  • First-time motorhome buyers
  • A good balance of space and easy driving
  • Weekend warriors who need real sleeping room
$75K - $200K+ | New MSRP Range

Common Questions

Things People Ask Me All the Time

After 22 years I've heard just about every question. Here are the ones that come up on almost every lot walk.

What is the difference between Class A, Class B, and Class C motorhomes?

+
Short version: Class A is the biggest — built on a bus-style chassis, 26 to 45 feet, maximum living space, harder to drive in tight spots. Class B is a camper van, 17 to 23 feet, parks anywhere, drives like a big van, limited on space. Class C is in the middle — 20 to 33 feet, built on a truck or van cutaway, has that over-cab bunk area, easier to drive than a Class A but more livable than a Class B. Most families I work with end up in a Class C. Couples who want something nimble usually go Class B. People who are serious about extended trips or full-timing tend to step up to a Class A. Come by and I'll walk you through each one in person — it makes a lot more sense once you're standing inside them.

Do I need a special license to drive a motorhome in Wisconsin?

+
Nope. Your standard Wisconsin Class D driver's license covers any motorhome for personal use, Class A through C, no matter the size or weight. No CDL required. What I do tell first-timers is to practice in a big empty parking lot before you head north on I-43. Especially if you're stepping into a 35-foot Class A for the first time — it's a different feel than anything you've driven before. Give yourself an hour in a quiet lot and you'll be fine.

How much does it cost to maintain a motorhome?

+
Budget somewhere between $1,000 and $3,000 a year for routine stuff — oil changes, tire checks, generator service, roof seal inspection, and winterization. Winterizing properly every fall is the one I see people skip, and it's also the one that leads to the most expensive repairs come spring. Our service department here in Sturtevant handles all of it, and we can get you on a maintenance schedule so nothing sneaks up on you. The rigs that last 20 years are the ones that get taken care of every season.

What should I look for when buying a used motorhome?

+
The thing that kills used motorhomes is water — so start at the roof. Look at every seal around every vent and seam. Check the ceiling inside for soft spots or any discoloration. After that, check the generator hours (think of it like mileage on an engine), look at tire age — tires can be cracked and dangerous even if the tread looks fine — and run every appliance and slide-out you can find. On our used rigs here, everything goes through a thorough inspection before it hits the lot. I'm not going to sell you something I wouldn't put my own family in. But if you're looking at a used unit somewhere else, those are the things I'd check first.
